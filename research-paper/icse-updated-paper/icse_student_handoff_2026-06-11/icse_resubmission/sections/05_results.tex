\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Unless stated otherwise, the main cross-model comparisons in this section use the 732 kernel-device rows completed by all three models in both prompt settings.
That avoids coverage bias from the incomplete \gptoss runs while preserving paired source-versus-SASS comparisons.

\subsection{RQ1: How Accurate Is Execution-Free \rai Triage Across the Corpus?}

\textbf{Answer.}
Off-the-shelf LLMs already provide useful execution-free triage for many nonzero FP32/FP64 cases, but source-only FP16 prediction is effectively at the trivial baseline.

\input{tables/results_summary_shared}

Table~\ref{tab:shared-results-summary} summarizes the main released comparison subset.
Two points are immediate.
First, all three source-only FP16 configurations collapse to the trivial regime: every model has 100\% median nonzero APE, and both \gptfivefour and \opus sit at 0.50 balanced accuracy for nonzero \bandwidthbound/\computebound triage.
This is indistinguishable from always predicting the memory side.
Second, source-only FP32/FP64 results are already materially better for the frontier models.
On the shared rows, \opus reaches median nonzero APE of 43.9\% for FP32 and 52.0\% for FP64, with balanced triage accuracy of 0.94 and 0.76 respectively.
\gptfivefour is weaker but still usable for triage at 59.2\%/74.5\% median nonzero APE and 0.89/0.67 balanced accuracy.
Even the weakest source-only FP32/FP64 configurations substantially outperform the trivial global-median-\rai heuristic, whose median APE is effectively 100\% on the shared subset.

The zero-aware tasks tell the same story.
Source-only FP16 zero-versus-nonzero detection stays near the trivial 0.50 baseline for all three models (0.52 for \gptfivefour, 0.51 for \gptoss, and 0.52 for \opus).
By contrast, source-only FP32/FP64 zero detection is already strong, reaching 0.86/0.89 balanced accuracy for \gptfivefour and 0.85/0.88 for \opus.
The stricter three-way \{zero, \bandwidthbound, \computebound\} view is lower, as expected, but it preserves the same ranking:
source-only FP16 remains near the 0.33 trivial baseline, while source-only FP32/FP64 rise to 0.82/0.68 for \gptfivefour and 0.85/0.73 for \opus.
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:shared-class-balance} makes the shared-subset skew explicit:
FP16/FP32/FP64 contain 513/496/623 zero rows out of 732, leaving only 34/50/53 compute-bound rows.
That is why the balanced task splits matter most in FP16 and FP64, where raw accuracy would largely reflect the zero majority rather than the usefulness of the triage signal.

Those gains are not explained by a simple lexical rule, but they are also not the only source-level baseline worth taking seriously.
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:static-baselines} adds both deterministic and lightweight learned static references.
The grouped runtime$\times$GPU median baseline already captures some of the zero-heavy FP16 structure (0.790 Task~A and 0.535 Task~D), but it collapses to the trivial 0.50/0.333 regime on FP32/FP64, so simple execution-context priors do not explain the frontier source-only results.
The deterministic source heuristic stays near trivial:
it reaches Task~D balanced accuracy of only 0.444 for FP32 and 0.531 for FP64, with median nonzero APE near 100\% on both precisions.
The grouped source learned baselines show that source-only FP16 collapse is not inevitable for a supervised static method:
the source random-forest and extra-trees rows reach 0.688--0.699 Task~C and 0.691--0.697 Task~D in FP16, far above the one-shot source-only LLM results.
They are still poor numeric regressors, however, with 141.6--217.4\% FP16 median nonzero APE and 100\% median nonzero APE on FP32/FP64.
That is better read as corpus-level triage learning than as accurate numeric \rai prediction.
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:seed-sensitivity} shows that the learned-baseline ranking is stable across five seeds: Task~C standard deviations stay below 0.034.
On FP32/FP64 binary triage, the frontier source-only LLMs remain at least competitive and often stronger.

Figure~\ref{fig:nonzero-ape} shows the same pattern distributionally.
The nonzero error distributions are extremely heavy-tailed, which is why we emphasize medians, balanced accuracy, and explicit task splits.
The figure also makes it clear that \gptoss should be read as a low-cost operating point rather than a full substitute for the frontier models: its source-only FP32/FP64 behavior is not catastrophic, but its FP16 behavior remains pinned at the ceiling and its completion coverage is materially lower.

The GPU and runtime effects are real, but they are uneven rather than universal.
The appendix GPU-stratified and runtime-stratified plots show lower FP32/FP64 absolute errors on A100/H100 than on 3080/A10, consistent with the original observation that smaller-cache GPUs make DRAM-visible traffic harder to infer.
The runtime split also remains precision-sensitive: CUDA tends to be easier for FP16/FP32 triage, while FP64 behavior is more mixed across CUDA and OpenMP.

\textbf{Practical implication.}
If only source-level evidence is available, off-the-shelf prompting is already a plausible early triage tool for many FP32/FP64 cases, but not for FP16.
Any tool that uses source-only LLM triage should therefore surface its FP16 uncertainty explicitly rather than treating all precisions symmetrically.

\subsection{RQ2: Does Post-Compilation Information Improve Triage?}

\textbf{Answer.}
Yes, decisively for FP16 and materially for the frontier models; the effect is smaller and less consistent for FP32/FP64, and much weaker for \gptoss.

The source-versus-SASS differences are best read as paired deltas on nonzero rows.
For \opus, \sourcesass lowers median nonzero FP16 APE by 78.9 points and raises nonzero balanced triage accuracy from 0.50 to 0.984.
For \gptfivefour, the corresponding FP16 triage lift is from 0.50 to 0.870, with 68.9\% of paired nonzero FP16 rows improving.
These gains are exactly what we would expect when source-visible semantics hide compiler-injected FP16 work.

The FP32/FP64 story is more nuanced.
For \opus, \sourcesass still improves median nonzero APE from 43.9\% to 18.3\% in FP32 and from 52.0\% to 15.3\% in FP64, but the balanced \bandwidthbound/\computebound lift is much smaller because source-only triage was already strong.
\gptfivefour also improves numerically in FP32/FP64, but less dramatically.
\gptoss shows almost no meaningful FP16 response to SASS and only mixed FP32/FP64 changes.

The paired evidence is stronger than the aggregate medians alone suggest.
On paired nonzero rows, exact sign tests favor \sourcesass over \sourceonly for every frontier-model precision setting:
for \gptfivefour, $p=4.7\times 10^{-12}$, $2.5\times 10^{-3}$, and $1.1\times 10^{-3}$ across FP16/FP32/FP64;
for \opus, $p=9.7\times 10^{-15}$, $2.5\times 10^{-4}$, and $1.8\times 10^{-4}$.
For the largest FP16 effects, paired bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals on the median absolute-percent-error reduction are [0.8,\,26.9] for \gptfivefour and [54.7,\,87.5] for \opus.
The FP64 intervals remain positive for \opus ([0.01,\,30.5]) and touch zero for \gptfivefour ([0.0,\,23.8]), while FP32 intervals are narrower and often pinned near zero despite significant sign tests.
Exact McNemar tests on Task~C likewise confirm the FP16 classification lift for \gptfivefour and \opus ($p=3.8\times 10^{-3}$ and $7.1\times 10^{-6}$), while FP32/FP64 paired classification gains are smaller and not consistently significant because source-only triage is already strong there.

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:static-baselines} also shows that post-compilation artifacts carry real signal even without an LLM.
The SASS mnemonic baseline already lifts zero/nonzero and three-way triage, and the learned SASS tree baselines reach 0.737--0.739 Task~C balanced accuracy in FP32 and 0.744--0.824 in FP64.
On SASS-backed FP64 binary triage the random-forest baseline exceeds the frontier LLMs, and the mnemonic baseline is even stronger in the budgeted retrieval setting, suggesting that compact post-compilation counts already capture much of that regime.
But those same static baselines remain weak on FP16 (best Task~C 0.591) and poor numeric regressors in FP16/FP32, so they do not eliminate the need for richer reasoning where compiler-realized work and memory effects are harder to reduce to counts alone.
The frontier LLMs remain decisively strongest on \sourcesass FP16 and still lead the main FP32 triage comparison.

This is the central methodological lesson of the paper.
Post-compilation evidence matters most when the source no longer exposes the computation that the profiler ultimately counts.
SASS is not universally required, but it is often the difference between trivial and useful FP16 triage.

\textbf{Practical implication.}
There are really two workflows here.
\sourceonly is the right deployment point for pre-compilation review and design-time triage.
\sourcesass is the right deployment point when compilation for the target architecture is possible but execution or profiling is still unavailable.
Tools should not collapse those workflows into a single ``hardware-free'' claim.

\subsection{RQ3: Where Do Frontier LLMs Beat Cheap Static Alternatives, and Where Do They Not?}

\textbf{Answer.}
Frontier LLMs dominate the main FP32 triage regimes and \sourcesass FP16, but cheap static methods already match or exceed point estimates in \sourceonly FP16 and \sourcesass FP64.

\input{tables/llm_vs_static_task_c}

Table~\ref{tab:llm-vs-static-task-c} makes the regime boundaries explicit by comparing the best off-the-shelf LLM and the best static reference separately within each prompt/precision cell.
The strongest paired LLM wins are \sourceonly FP32 (0.940 versus 0.815, 44 versus 3 discordant wins, $p<0.001$), \sourcesass FP16 (0.984 versus 0.591, 35 versus 5, $p<0.001$), and \sourcesass FP32 (0.967 versus 0.739, 46 versus 2, $p<0.001$).
These are exactly the regimes where richer reasoning about lowered arithmetic and DRAM-visible traffic appears to matter most.

The static side is still real.
In \sourceonly FP16, the best grouped source baseline reaches 0.699 Task~C balanced accuracy while every source-only LLM remains at the 0.50 trivial baseline.
In \sourcesass FP64, the best learned SASS baseline reaches 0.824 versus 0.765 for the best LLM.
Those are point-estimate advantages rather than statistically decisive paired wins on the shared subset (exact $p=0.210$ and $p=0.238$), which is unsurprising given that FP16 and FP64 contain only 34 and 53 compute-bound rows respectively.
Still, together with the budget-sweep table, they show that some regimes are already recoverable from cheap static features after lowering.

Numeric \rai estimation remains the sharper separator.
Even where static baselines are competitive for triage, their nonzero median APE remains much larger than the strongest frontier LLM results in the corresponding cells:
217\% versus 100\% for \sourceonly FP16, 100\% versus 43.9\% for \sourceonly FP32, and 67.1\% versus 15.3\% for \sourcesass FP64.
The static baselines therefore look better as queueing or routing components than as direct numeric \rai estimators.

\textbf{Practical implication.}
The right takeaway is not ``LLMs beat static methods everywhere'' or the reverse.
A practical tool should route by regime:
cheap static filters are credible for \sourceonly FP16 and some \sourcesass FP64 cases, while frontier LLM reasoning is most justified for \sourcesass FP16 and the main FP32 triage cells.

\subsection{RQ4: What Does the Signal Buy Under a Profiling Budget?}

\textbf{Answer.}
Ranking rows by predicted \rai relative to the balance point can move many true compute-bound kernels to the front of a fixed profiling queue, especially for FP16 with \sourcesass and for FP32 even in \sourceonly mode.

\input{tables/budget_recall_at_10}

Table~\ref{tab:budget-recall-at-10} makes the scarce-resource scenario explicit.
If a tool profiles only the top 10\% of kernel-device rows per precision, random ranking would recover 10\% of the true compute-bound rows by construction.
On the shared subset, \sourcesass \gptfivefour and \opus recover all FP16 compute-bound rows at that budget, while their source-only variants recover none.
FP32 is already strong source-only: the top 10\% of \gptfivefour rows recovers 82\% of true compute-bound cases, and \opus reaches 96\%.
FP64 remains harder, plateauing around 0.55--0.59 recall at the same budget.

The selected queues are also strongly enriched relative to random.
At the same 10\% budget, \sourcesass FP16 queues for \gptfivefour and \opus are about 9.9$\times$ richer in true compute-bound rows than random, and source-only FP32 queues for \gptfivefour and \opus are about 8.1$\times$ and 9.5$\times$ richer.
This is the main practical reading of the paper's signal:
it is useful not because it replaces profiling, but because it can prioritize which kernels reach the profiler first.

The 10\% point is not the whole story.
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:budget-recall-sweep} shows that the ranking is broadly stable across 5/10/20/30\% budgets, and it sharpens the comparison to the static baselines.
For source-only FP16 queueing, the best learned static baseline is already stronger than any source-only LLM, reaching 0.471/0.765/0.971/1.000 recall across the sweep.
For \sourcesass FP16, the frontier LLM remains clearly better, with \opus already at 0.971 recall at 5\% budget and 1.000 by 10\%.
For source-only FP32, \opus remains strongest at the front of the queue.
For \sourcesass FP64, a cheap SASS mnemonic ranker outperforms the LLMs across the whole sweep, again showing that some post-compilation regimes are largely recoverable from deterministic counts alone.
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:compute-bound-prf} shows that these rankings are not merely high-recall because the models predict compute-bound everywhere.
On FP32, source-only \opus reaches compute-bound precision/recall/F1 of 1.000/0.880/0.936, and \sourcesass raises that to 0.979/0.940/0.959.
The FP16 failure mode is much sharper:
every source-only model has zero compute-bound recall, while \gptoss \sourcesass reaches only 0.176 recall despite 0.750 precision.
This is why we treat source-only FP16 triage as unusable and the frontier \sourcesass FP16 lift as practically meaningful rather than cosmetic.

That practical value still has a price.
The strongest models are also much more expensive, but the latency ranking is not monotonic.

Table~\ref{tab:shared-results-summary} reports median per-query cost and latency on the shared subset.
\gptoss remains orders of magnitude cheaper than the frontier models: roughly \$0.0006 per source-only query and \$0.0008 with SASS, versus \$0.0197/\$0.0315 for \gptfivefour and \$0.0679/\$0.1066 for \opus.
Latency is different: \gptfivefour is the fastest median response at roughly 11.3\,s source-only and 12.3\,s with SASS, while \gptoss is the slowest at about 33\,s in both settings.

\textbf{Practical implication.}
The strongest deployment is a cascade, not an LLM-only stack.
Cheap static rankers already cover source-only FP16 and \sourcesass FP64 surprisingly well, while frontier LLMs justify their cost where source-visible semantics are insufficient, especially for \sourcesass FP16 and the main FP32 triage regimes.
